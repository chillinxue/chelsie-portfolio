01

Background — Making fashion discovery feel less overwhelming

Shopbop, an Amazon company, wanted to explore more social and interactive shopping experiences that could increase engagement and strengthen product discovery. Working with Amazon Shopbop engineering mentors, our team explored a new way for customers to discover fashion based on their personal taste.

What if discovering an outfit felt more like expressing your taste than searching through a catalog?

Instead of asking users to browse hundreds of products, we designed Bop & Browse, a swipe-based experience where customers quickly react to clothing and outfits while the product learns their preferences.

02

The problem — Too many choices, not enough confidence

Our research showed that fashion shoppers weren't struggling with a lack of options — they were struggling with too many. Users described endless scrolling as overwhelming, found it hard to imagine how pieces would work together as an outfit, and wanted recommendations that reflected their occasion, budget and style rather than generic "similar products."

How might we reduce decision fatigue while helping shoppers discover complete outfits that feel personal, inspiring, and easy to act on?

03

My role

As Product & UX Lead, I helped drive the project from problem definition through research, prototyping, validation, and product recommendations — contributing to product requirements and feature prioritization, competitive and customer research, user journey and information architecture, sprint planning and backlog management, wireframing and interactive prototyping, user interviews and usability testing, and translating findings into roadmap recommendations.

04

Research & problem framing

We focused on fashion-conscious online shoppers who wanted to look put-together quickly without spending significant time assembling outfits themselves. Our users valued:

Speed

Find relevant outfits without endless scrolling.

Personalization

Recommendations should reflect their actual taste.

Confidence

Understand how individual pieces work together.

Context

Shop based on occasions such as work, travel, dates or events.

Discovery

Still encounter new styles, not just past purchases.

Personalization shouldn't only predict what users already like. It should help them discover what they might like next.

05

Product hypothesis

Rather than starting with a large feature set, we identified one key assumption to test: users will prefer curated, swipe-based fashion discovery over endless scrolling, while still wanting control over individual items within an outfit. The swipe interaction reduced the effort required to communicate preference, and every interaction created a lightweight signal to improve recommendations.

Browse→React→Learn→Personalize→Discover

06

Design process — From an open-ended idea to a testable product

We followed an iterative process rather than jumping into high-fidelity design: understanding the challenge and existing behavior, defining a narrower problem, ideating across feeds, grids, filters and swipe interactions, prototyping around the riskiest assumption, testing with 11 users across different ages, professions and locations, and iterating the backlog directly from findings.

07

What we learned — The concept worked, but users wanted more control

Testing validated the core idea: users found swipe-based discovery more fun and engaging than endless browsing, and liked teaching the system what they liked. But research also challenged some assumptions — users wanted:

Bigger visuals

Fashion is visual; imagery needed to dominate the experience.

Item-level control

Users might like a jacket but dislike the skirt in the same outfit.

Occasion-based discovery

"Wedding guest" or "travel" was more useful than abstract categories.

Flexible personalization

Worried that one dislike might permanently remove a whole style.

Clearer onboarding

Too many preference questions made it feel like work.

08

Key features

Swipe-based discovery

Like or Nope on individual pieces, generating explicit preference signals.

Preference onboarding

Users choose interests directly instead of relying only on purchase history.

Smart filters

Narrow discovery by occasion, season, style, brand, fit and price.

Personal closet

Liked items are saved into a persistent, revisitable space.

Outfit match

Saved preferences combine into complete outfit recommendations.

Item-level control

"I like this top, but not those shoes" — nuance beyond all-or-nothing outfits.

09

Building through Agile sprints

The project was managed through iterative Agile sprints in Jira. Each sprint began with backlog review, acceptance criteria, and dependency mapping; weekly stand-ups surfaced blockers, and user interviews and prototype testing were built into the cycle so findings could immediately shape the next backlog.

Research→Build→Test→Learn→Prioritize→Iterate

10

Product opportunities

Swipe behavior wasn't only useful for individual recommendations. Aggregated preference signals could inform merchandising (trending products and combinations), marketing and styling (emerging preference clusters), and personalization across home feeds, email and push — making the concept a feedback loop between customer behavior and Shopbop's broader recommendation ecosystem.

Reflection — The best personalization gives users control

I initially thought the core value would be making fashion discovery faster. Research showed something more interesting: people want personalization, but they also want to understand and influence how it works. This project reinforced three principles:

  1. Validate the riskiest assumption first. A polished solution is useless if the underlying behavior isn't real.
  2. Behavior is often more valuable than stated preference. Every interaction can become a signal for the next experience.
  3. Personalization should adapt, not restrict. The best systems help users discover something new while keeping them in control.

Redesigning the end-to-end library experience from book discovery to borrowing and management.

01

Background — Turning a fragmented website into a simpler mobile journey

The Madison Public Library website provides access to books, events, and community resources, but its existing experience created friction around common tasks. Users struggled to find books, understand event registration, and navigate unclear categories — and basic library activities were scattered across the site rather than organized around what people were actually trying to do.

How might we make borrowing, picking up, renewing, and returning books easier while helping users discover more of what the library offers?

The goal wasn't to shrink the website for mobile. It was to redesign the entire library journey around user tasks.

02

The problem — Borrowing should feel like one journey, not several disconnected tasks

A library experience doesn't end when someone finds a book. Users need to understand whether it's available, which library has it, where to pick it up, when it's due, and whether they can renew it. Search, availability, account management and library information weren't presented as one continuous workflow.

How can the product support the complete lifecycle of borrowing a book — from discovery to return?

03

My role

As the UX and Product Designer, I owned the redesign from early problem framing through the final interactive experience: identifying usability and navigation problems, defining the core product problem, mapping the end-to-end borrowing journey, creating storyboards and task flows, designing paper prototypes, conducting usability testing, and iterating information architecture, wireframes and final UI.

04

Design principles — Design around intent, not organizational structure

01. Personalized library experience

Help users discover relevant books without needing to know exactly what to search for.

02. Easier borrowing & management

Connect search, availability, pickup, history and renewal into one clear workflow.

03. Library events & services

Make the broader value of the public library easier to discover.

05

Mapping the user journey

Before designing screens, I designed the journey — storyboarding a scenario where a user needs a book, discovers it's available at another location, chooses a pickup, and later tracks or returns it. This exposed a key insight: book discovery and book management shouldn't be treated as separate experiences. They are different moments in the same journey. From this I mapped three task flows.

Flow 01 — Search & borrow

Home→Search→Results→Book details→Sign in→Select pickup→Confirmation

Flow 02 — Personalized discovery

Home→Sign in→Profile→Recommendations→Book details→Borrow

Flow 03 — Borrowing history & tracking

Home→Sign in→Dashboard→Borrowing history→Book details

06

Prototype & testing — Testing the structure before polishing the interface

Before high-fidelity UI, I built a paper prototype to test whether the information architecture and task flows made sense, asking users to: search for and borrow a book, borrow through personalized recommendations, and find and track borrowing information. This surfaced structural problems early, before time was spent polishing the interface.

07

What I learned — A feature can exist and still be effectively invisible

Testing revealed an important distinction between functionality and discoverability. Most screens were understandable, but users struggled with the Personal Page — not because features like Recommended Books and Book Tracking were missing, but because the labels and hierarchy made them hard to find.

Would a user know where to look for it without being taught?

08

Navigation matters more than expected

On the Book Details screen, users expected Holdings and Location to expose relevant library and location information, and some exited the experience instead of using the intended Back navigation. These behaviors pointed to the product needing stronger information scent, navigation hierarchy, interaction consistency and visual hierarchy — usability often breaks not from one major missing feature, but from several small moments of uncertainty.

09

Key features

Unified book search

A mobile-first search that surfaces the information users need before borrowing.

Availability & pickup

Book details connect discovery directly to fulfillment — not just "it exists," but "how do I get it."

Personalized recommendations

A second discovery model alongside search, for "help me find something I might like."

Personal library dashboard

Turns the account area into a task-oriented hub instead of a list of settings.

Borrowing history & tracking

Extends the product beyond checkout into ongoing book management.

10

From paper to product

Problem definition→Brainstorming→Storyboard→Task flows→Paper prototype→User testing→Wireframes→Final UI

The screen design process refined both the interface and the underlying workflow, rather than treating visual design as a final layer added after product decisions. The final prototype preserved all three core journeys throughout.

11

Outcomes

Testing validated the overall task structure while revealing navigation and labeling issues that directly informed the next design iteration. Users were generally able to navigate the main experience, while testing exposed specific discoverability problems around recommendations, tracking, and the Personal Page.

Reflection — Good UX is often about removing uncertainty

At first, I focused on making the library experience more convenient by bringing key functions into a mobile app. Usability testing showed convenience isn't simply about fewer screens or more features — users need to understand where they are, what they can do, and what happens next.

  1. Design journeys, not isolated screens. Search, borrowing, pickup and management are stages of the same user goal.
  2. Test structure before visual polish. Paper prototypes surfaced navigation problems before they became expensive decisions.
  3. Use user behavior to challenge design assumptions. When users navigated differently than expected, the fix was the interface, not teaching the user.
